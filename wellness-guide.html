Your wellness journey starts here.

Discover how to enjoy sauna sessions, explore cold-water experiences and make more time for recovery. From your first visit to your favourite weekly ritual, you'll find practical tips you can use with confidence.

Before you begin: This guide is general information, not medical advice. Heat and cold exposure can affect blood pressure, breathing and heart function. If you're pregnant, have a heart or circulation condition, take medication that affects heat tolerance or blood pressure, or have another medical concern, check with a healthcare professional first. Stop immediately if you feel faint, dizzy, confused, unusually breathless or unwell. Cold plunges are optional.

01 · GETTING STARTED

Enjoy your first sauna session

A sauna is a heated room designed for relaxing and sweating. Traditional saunas use heated stones and dry heat, sometimes with water added to create steam. Temperatures and humidity vary widely between venues; always follow the operator's guidance.

Before you go

  • Drink water normally throughout the day, and avoid arriving dehydrated.
  • Bring swimwear if required, two towels, water, and sandals if the venue recommends them.
  • Avoid alcohol before and during a session. Don't go in if you feel ill, feverish or light-headed.
  • Tell staff if it's your first visit and ask about temperature, exits and house rules.

A simple beginner-friendly approach

  1. Settle in: Choose a lower bench if available, sit comfortably and breathe normally.
  2. Keep it short: Try around 5–10 minutes to begin with, or less if the heat feels intense. There is no minimum time you must achieve.
  3. Step out: Cool down gradually in fresh air or a comfortable-temperature shower. Sit and rest.
  4. Rehydrate: Drink water and see how you feel before deciding whether to do another short round.

Remember: You don't have to stay until a timer runs out. Leaving early is always the right choice if you feel uncomfortable. Avoid falling asleep in the sauna.

02 · COLD & RECOVERY

Discover cold-water experiences

Cold-water immersion means placing part or all of your body in cold water. Some people enjoy the invigorating sensation or use it after exercise. But sudden immersion can trigger a cold-shock response: gasping, rapid breathing and a sharp rise in heart rate and blood pressure. That can be dangerous, particularly for people with underlying health conditions.

Safer ways to explore cooling down

  • Start with fresh air or a lukewarm shower. An ice bath is never essential.
  • If you choose a managed cold plunge, ask the operator about water temperature, supervision, hygiene and safe entry and exit.
  • Enter slowly and keep your head above water. Never jump in, force yourself to stay or compete over time.
  • Keep breathing naturally. Never hyperventilate or hold your breath in or near water.
  • Get out immediately if you feel numb, dizzy, panicked, confused or unable to control your breathing.
  • Dry off and warm up gently afterwards.

Open water is different: Sea and lake swimming add currents, depth, weather and rescue risks. Never swim alone; choose a supervised setting and follow local safety guidance. Cold water can be dangerous even for strong swimmers.

03 · YOUR RITUAL

Create your hot-and-cold ritual

Alternating sauna and cold water is often called contrast bathing. It can feel refreshing, but it is not a required part of sauna use and is not automatically safer or more beneficial than enjoying the sauna alone.

A gentle, optional routine

  1. Begin with a short, comfortable sauna visit.
  2. Step out and allow your breathing and heart rate to settle.
  3. Cool down in fresh air or a comfortable-temperature shower.
  4. Rest, hydrate and decide whether you want another round. Skip the plunge if you're unsure.

Rapid transitions from very hot to very cold conditions can put extra strain on the cardiovascular system. If you have cardiovascular risk factors or feel unwell, avoid extreme temperature changes and seek medical advice.

04 · WHAT THE SCIENCE SAYS

The benefits of sauna and cold water

Relaxation and wellbeing

Many people find sauna time relaxing and enjoyable. It can provide a quiet break from busy routines, especially when paired with rest and time away from screens.

Heart and circulation

Some observational research links frequent sauna use with better cardiovascular outcomes. These studies do not prove that sauna use causes the benefit, and results may not apply to everyone. Sauna is not a replacement for exercise, medication or medical treatment.

Muscle soreness and exercise recovery

Cold-water immersion may reduce perceived muscle soreness after some exercise sessions. Evidence varies by activity, temperature and timing. Regular cold immersion immediately after resistance training may interfere with some muscle-growth adaptations, so more is not always better.

Understanding the bigger picture

Claims that sauna or ice baths “detox” the body, cure illness, dramatically boost immunity or guarantee weight loss go beyond good evidence. Most short-term weight change after a sauna is fluid loss, not fat loss.

05 · LOOK AFTER YOURSELF

Feel your best: hydration and recovery

  • Before: Eat and drink normally; don't start a session dehydrated or after drinking alcohol.
  • During: Take breaks whenever needed. Keep water accessible and follow venue rules about bottles in hot rooms.
  • After: Drink to replace fluids lost through sweating. Longer or especially sweaty sessions may require additional fluids and electrolytes; needs vary.
  • Listen to your body: Headache, nausea, dizziness, weakness or unusual fatigue are reasons to stop and cool down.
  • Make room for rest: A comfortable shower, dry clothes, a snack and a few quiet minutes can be the best part of your ritual.

Seek urgent medical help for chest pain, fainting, severe breathing difficulty or persistent confusion.

06 · FEEL AT HOME

Feel confident at any sauna

  • Check booking times, swimwear requirements and age rules before arriving.
  • Shower beforehand if the venue asks you to.
  • Sit on a towel and keep shared spaces clean.
  • Respect other guests' space, conversation preferences and privacy.
  • Never take photographs of other guests without their permission.
  • Ask staff before adding water to stones or using essential oils — some venues prohibit both.
  • Follow posted instructions and let staff know about any safety concern.

07 · COMMON QUESTIONS

Your questions, answered

How long should a beginner stay in a sauna?

Start with a short session, perhaps 5–10 minutes or less, and leave whenever you feel uncomfortable. Venue conditions and personal tolerance vary; longer is not automatically better.

Do I need an ice bath after a sauna?

No. Fresh air, rest or a comfortable-temperature shower is enough to cool down. Cold plunges are optional.

Should I do a sauna if I'm feeling sick?

It's best to skip it if you have a fever, feel unwell or are dehydrated. Ask a healthcare professional if you're unsure.

Can I sauna while pregnant?

Overheating can be a concern during pregnancy. Seek personalised advice from your maternity healthcare team before using a sauna or cold plunge.

Is it safe to plunge into the sea after a sauna?

Sea swimming adds cold shock, currents, tides and other hazards. Don't treat it like a supervised plunge pool. Check conditions, never go alone and follow water-safety guidance.
